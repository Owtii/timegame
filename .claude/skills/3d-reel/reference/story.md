# Story: topics, hooks, scripts and series

## When they ask for ideas

The first reel started with a run of requests:
1. "Give me a good informative topic about something that we use everyday but dont know how it actually works."
2. "Something very niche but something we really use."
3. "Mix it with something interesting that never would happen but what if."
4. "Give me more exciting ones."

They picked **"What if every phone in your city called someone at the same time?"** The reel then told it through a real event: the August 23, 2011 earthquake in Washington, D.C., when the phone network jammed.

The second reel started with "Same style but I need a new video. Give me video ideas maybe something with a story and scary story", then "a scary story with a plot twist... 1 min long... at the end a plot twist with no explanation and the video just ends."

How to answer a request for ideas:
- Give **5 to 8 options**, each with a one-line hook the viewer would hear first and the twist or payoff in a few words.
- Make them concrete and a little impossible ("what if"), tied to something everyone knows (phones, lifts, night shifts).
- Then **wait** for their pick. Don't build anything yet.
- "Dont change anything yet, give me suggestions" means options only. They often answer with just a number ("use 2").

## Hooks

The first seconds decide everything. They asked for "a very catchy hook" on the first reel, and the hook of Part 1 was reworked three times.

- The hook raises a question and hints at danger without explaining it. Part 1 settled on: **"Everyone warned me about the night shift. Nobody warned me about my coworker."** The reason was so people won't know at first that the coworker is a weird entity, while sensing he isn't safe.
- It's short and has no pause inside it ("make the hook now a little less long... because it has weird pause in between and at the end").
- It comes with a cinematic opening shot ("make the beginning a cinematic shot for the hook"), not a static frame.
- **The first 1 to 5 seconds must hold the viewer, every reel and every part, the recap included** ("the first part must also be a hook, so the viewer keeps watching the first 1 to 5 sec"). In the first seconds:
  - a striking image within the first second: never more than a moment of black, never a calm establishing shot;
  - a sound within the first 0.2 s, and something loud or strange within 3 s;
  - words on screen within the first second: a question, a stake or a hint of danger;
  - nothing that explains first. A recap can come second, after the jolt.
- For a series part, the recap must hook too: open on its scariest beat, or on the picked answer, not on calm setup. Offer the user options before changing an opening ("give suggestions first").

## The explainer shape (The Day the Phones Jammed)

- **Hook** (0–4 s): the problem in one image, e.g. full signal but the call won't connect.
- **The real story** (date, place, what people did), then **inside the system** (why it happens), then **the fix** ("Text, don't call"), then a closing beat.
- Explain it so "everyone should understand it", with visuals "understandable by just looking and hearing the narrator and seeing the text". Narration, picture and on-screen text say the same thing at the same moment.
- Tone: "informative and simple but a good story", told like a story and not like an AI summary. Each sentence must make sense on its own.
- Chapters with short titles (the player lists them), about 5–10 s each.

## The scary-story shape (The 13th Floor, The Graveyard Shift)

- **About a minute**, first person, told afterwards by a narrator ("I got home at 2 a.m."). Each scene adds a little more until the twist.
- **A building-up script**: an ordinary setting, one small wrong detail, then more wrong details, a scare, an escape, and false safety.
- **A twist at the end with no explanation, and the video just ends.** Cut to black, often mid-sentence and with no sound: "I took out my phone… to call the po—", then the notice "Elevator is out of service. Please use the stairs." Hold the black for a second.
- **People behave the way a normal person would.**
  - After seeing something terrifying he doesn't go to bed: he hides behind the bed and tries to call the police.
  - The phone was always in his hand, never left on the bed.
  - A new night guard sitting at a desk in the middle of a hallway isn't believable, so it became a lobby with a security counter.
- **Every action has a reason you can see.** He gets angry because he can't find what he's looking for, and he hits things for that reason ("not just randomly hitting").
- **Subtle lines beat lists.** "My key, my jacket, my shoes, my mug" was "too much, something subtle". Keep the specifics in the picture.
- **The ending of The 13th Floor** went through three versions:
  1. An official statement that the lift has been under maintenance since yesterday.
  2. Hiding and the phone notice.
  3. That, plus the bathroom light and shower coming on mid-sentence and the door creeping open, then the cut.

  The last one was best because the danger arrives while he's reading.

## Series with a choice (The Graveyard Shift)

**Why:** "what could we do to make people comment like they have a choice, and something they get hooked on". Each part ends on a question with two options, viewers comment their pick, and the next part follows the winning choice.

- **The end of a part:**
  1. The picture freezes on the edge of the reveal: the faceplate coming round, the shadow over the desk.
  2. The background blurs and a quiet horror tone holds attention.
  3. The choice card shows **one** question ("at the end only one question"): "What would you do?" with two short options, such as TURN AROUND or RUN, or RUN FOR THE EXIT or STAY HIDDEN.
  4. Hold it about 2 s longer than feels necessary ("make the question on the end a bit longer 2sec longer").
- **The start of the next part:**
  1. A very short recap with a tiny "Previously · Part 1" tag ("something small, so u understand"). No "previously on…" banner and no red dot.
  2. A few key shots from the last part, each long enough for its line, using **the last part's own sound** ("dont add ur own sound effects").
  3. Then the question with the picked answer ("You chose: run"), and straight into the action.
  4. Keep the recap short: "make the first part w part 1 a little less just a quick good hook".
- **Continuity:** the next part uses the same places, props and look ("dont change rooms or visuals that is different from part1"). Only the story changes, plus what time has done, like a suit more stained and dirty.
- **Character logic across parts:** keep a hidden logic the viewer can sense. Here the man in the suit is an entity that left his body and is searching for it, getting angrier because he can't find it, and he never talks. His actions in every part come from that.
- **When asked "what could he do besides run or turn around?"**, offer options that fit the logic (hide, call someone, fight, follow him) and let them choose.

## Scripts

The user writes scripts in this shape, and you should too when asked:

```
SCENE 2: THE ARRIVAL (0:06 – 0:16)
* Environment: ...
* Camera (POV): ...
* Audio: ...
* Narrator (Internal Monologue): "..."
* Transition: ...
```

- Time every scene. Keep the total near the target length and the narration speakable in that time (about 2.5 words per second with pauses).
- When asked, deliver the script as a downloadable text file (`The-Graveyard-Shift_Script_Part1-2.txt`) and keep it updated as the reel changes: timestamps, lines and the narrator's words.
- When they paste a script, build that script. Keep their lines, adapt only what the places or the length force, and say what you adapted.
- **No narration until they approve.** Build with captions only first: "dont add voice narrator yet till, I give u the permission to do it. because I need to see first then generate to save credits."

## Lines and captions

- The narrator tells it afterwards, plainly: "He just stared at me… then turned around and kept working."
- What I say in the moment is short and breathless: "The doors are locked…!", "Wait… this guy is the guy from the morning shift."
- A phone voice gets its own colour and its own sound (a phone filter).
- A caption describes exactly what's on screen while it shows. It stays a little mysterious ("something beside it caught his eye", not "he saw the photo"). It never claims something the picture contradicts: the user caught "made him freeze" over a shot where he keeps walking.
- When they rewrite a line ("this might be my chance.. something like this. dont say like or just stay"), use their words.
- Captions end before the cut or transition they belong to (see `craft.md`).
